# Accuracy and Sources

Accuracy is a core credibility requirement for AI Literacy Lab.

## Internal Standard

Curriculum claims should be one of the following:

- directly supported by a cited reputable source
- derived from a synthetic artifact where the answer can be checked internally
- clearly framed as a heuristic, design principle, or discussion prompt

Avoid making legal, medical, financial, employment, or regulatory claims unless the module is explicitly designed around sourced, jurisdiction-specific material and reviewed accordingly.

## Preferred Source Types

- NIST AI Risk Management Framework resources
- peer-reviewed human factors and human-computer interaction research
- recognized standards bodies
- official organizational guidance when discussing a specific framework
- primary-source documentation for any tool-specific claim

## Durable Source Anchors

- NIST AI RMF 1.0: voluntary, use-case-agnostic framework for managing AI risks and promoting trustworthy and responsible AI.
  - https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-ai-rmf-10
- NIST AI RMF Generative AI Profile: cross-sectoral companion profile for generative AI risk management.
  - https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence
- NIST AI Resource Center: implementation resources for AI testing, evaluation, verification, validation, and risk management.
  - https://airc.nist.gov/
- NIST Privacy Framework: voluntary tool for identifying and managing privacy risk while protecting individuals' privacy.
  - https://www.nist.gov/privacy-framework
- Automation bias literature: users can over-rely on automated decision support, reducing vigilance and independent information processing.
  - https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7651899/

## Module Claim Discipline

For each module, maintain four internal notes:

- Claim: what the curriculum teaches.
- Citation: why we believe it, if it is a factual claim.
- Learner move: what behavior should change.
- Caveat: what we are not claiming.

Example:

- Claim: AI outputs can be wrong even when they are fluent and confident.
- Citation: NIST generative AI risk framing and peer-reviewed automation bias research support the need for verification and human oversight.
- Learner move: break outputs into claims and check each important claim against evidence.
- Caveat: this does not mean every AI output is wrong or useless.

## Design Implication

For learner-facing scenarios, prefer synthetic evidence packets. They make the experience accurate, inspectable, timeless, and legally safer.

When the curriculum teaches a general principle, the activity should make that principle observable inside the exercise itself.

Example: instead of claiming that "AI often invents facts" in isolation, provide a synthetic AI output and source packet where learners can see which claims are supported, overstated, or unsupported.
