# Learning Design

## Core Thesis

AI literacy is applied judgment under uncertainty.

Learners need more than definitions. They need repeated practice noticing when AI output is useful, when it is fragile, what evidence is missing, what risks are present, who remains accountable, and what kind of review is appropriate.

## Pedagogical Commitments

### 1. Scenario Before Vocabulary

Each module should begin with a decision, artifact, or dilemma before formal explanation. Definitions are introduced after the learner has a reason to care.

Example pattern:

1. Present a plausible workplace situation.
2. Ask the learner to make an initial decision.
3. Reveal a complication.
4. Ask the learner to revise their reasoning.
5. Name the concept after the need for it has emerged.

### 2. Productive Struggle

The training should deliberately create moments where a reasonable first answer is incomplete. The point is not to trick learners. The point is to help them feel the gap between fluent AI output and justified reliance.

Good struggle is:

- relevant to real work
- bounded enough to solve
- followed by feedback
- connected to a transferable principle
- respectful of the learner

Bad struggle is:

- gotcha-based
- overly technical
- dependent on obscure facts
- impossible without domain expertise
- framed as learner failure

### 3. Zone of Proximal Development

Modules should sit just beyond what a thoughtful nontechnical learner can do unaided. Scaffolds should be available, but not immediate.

Useful scaffolds include:

- evidence checklists
- risk lenses
- comparison tables
- worked examples after an initial attempt
- hints that reveal progressively
- peer discussion prompts

### 4. Time-Resistant Design

The curriculum should avoid being anchored to a specific model, product interface, regulation snapshot, or news cycle unless an example is clearly labeled as replaceable.

Prefer durable concepts:

- uncertainty
- accountability
- incentives
- data sensitivity
- verification
- human review
- bias and representation
- risk tiering
- misuse and overreliance
- documentation

### 5. Assessment by Reasoning

Assessment should ask learners to explain, classify, revise, justify, or escalate. Multiple-choice questions can be useful, but they should test judgment rather than recall.

Better evidence of literacy:

- learner identifies missing evidence
- learner spots a plausible but unsupported claim
- learner classifies data sensitivity appropriately
- learner distinguishes low-risk drafting from high-risk decision support
- learner documents assumptions and review steps
- learner knows when not to use AI

### 6. Accuracy as a Design Constraint

The curriculum loses credibility if it teaches AI skepticism while making weak claims itself.

Every module should distinguish between:

- sourced factual claims
- synthetic scenario facts
- heuristics
- discussion prompts
- open questions

When possible, activities should use self-contained synthetic evidence packets so the learner can verify the answer inside the exercise.

## Reusable Module Pattern

Every module should include:

1. A realistic opening scenario
2. A learner decision or prediction
3. An AI output, policy artifact, dataset excerpt, message, or workflow sample
4. A complication that exposes the limits of the first answer
5. A short concept explanation
6. A second attempt or transfer scenario
7. A debrief
8. A rubric or evidence of learning

## Anti-Patterns

Avoid:

- slide-only exposition
- quizzes that reward memorized definitions
- tool tutorials masquerading as literacy
- fear-based messaging
- hype-based messaging
- pretending risk is only a legal or technical issue
- implying that prompt-writing skill equals AI literacy
- one-size-fits-all certificates with no performance evidence
