# Curriculum Map

## Audience

This curriculum is designed for broad, nontechnical professional audiences. It should work for individual learners, teams, educators, public sector staff, nonprofits, analysts, managers, and operational workers who need practical AI judgment.

## Module Map

| Module | Durable Question | Productive Struggle | Evidence of Learning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. What AI is good and bad at | When is AI useful, and when is it fragile? | Learners sort tasks by suitability, then discover that context, stakes, and verification change the answer. | Learner can explain why the same AI use may be acceptable in one context and inappropriate in another. |
| 2. Why confident answers can be wrong | Why does fluent output feel more reliable than it is? | Learners evaluate a polished answer with subtle unsupported claims. | Learner identifies missing evidence, false precision, and signs of overconfidence. |
| 3. Data, privacy, and confidentiality | What should not be entered, shared, or inferred? | Learners decide whether to use AI on realistic workplace material with mixed sensitivity. | Learner classifies information sensitivity and proposes safer alternatives. |
| 4. Bias, fairness, and representational harm | Who can be harmed by AI use, even without bad intent? | Learners compare outputs that appear neutral but treat groups or cases unevenly. | Learner identifies representational gaps, proxy variables, and fairness concerns. |
| 5. Human accountability and review | Who is responsible when AI contributes to work? | Learners must assign responsibility in a workflow where AI, employee, manager, and organization all play roles. | Learner distinguishes assistance from delegation and defines appropriate review. |
| 6. Using AI well in everyday work | How can AI improve work without replacing judgment? | Learners improve a weak AI-assisted workflow that overtrusts the model. | Learner designs a workflow with clear purpose, constraints, review, and documentation. |
| 7. Risk classification and escalation | Which uses need additional review or governance? | Learners classify ambiguous AI use cases and defend escalation choices. | Learner uses a risk lens consistently and can explain borderline cases. |
| 8. Capstone | Can the learner navigate a realistic AI decision from start to finish? | Learners handle a multi-step workplace simulation with changing information and tradeoffs. | Learner produces a defensible decision memo, review plan, or escalation recommendation. |

## Curriculum Spine

The modules should move from basic capability judgment to governance behavior:

1. Capability: What can AI help with?
2. Reliability: Why can AI be wrong?
3. Data: What can safely be shared?
4. Harm: Who could be affected?
5. Accountability: Who owns the decision?
6. Practice: How should AI fit into work?
7. Governance: When should risk be escalated?
8. Integration: Can the learner apply the full pattern?

## Assessment Philosophy

Each module should produce some observable evidence of learning:

- a classification
- a short explanation
- a revised decision
- a risk note
- a verification plan
- a policy-aware recommendation
- a reflection on what changed and why

Completion should mean the learner practiced judgment, not merely reached the final screen.
